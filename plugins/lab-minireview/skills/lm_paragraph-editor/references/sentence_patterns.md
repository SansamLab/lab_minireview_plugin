# Pass 2 — Sentence Styling Reference

This document defines the sentence-styling pass. Pass 2 takes the structurally-revised paragraph from Pass 1 (or the user's original paragraph if Pass 1 was skipped) and refines it at the sentence level — variety, emphasis, rhythm, verbs, and pattern-based revisions.

Do not rewrite the whole paragraph wholesale. Diagnose sentence structure first, then recommend selective, justified changes.

---

## Core principles

**Preserve meaning.** Do not add claims, mechanisms, or evidence. Do not remove qualifications unless they are redundant. Preserve uncertainty when required.

**Keep the main idea in the main clause.** If the main point is buried in a subordinate clause, modifier, appositive, or prepositional phrase, revise so that grammar reflects importance.

**Use stronger verbs.** Reduce unnecessary *to be* verbs. Avoid expletive openings such as *there is*, *there are*, *it is* when they delay the subject or create wordiness. Prefer precise verbs that name the action or relationship. The strength of a sentence usually lies in its verbs.

**Use emphasis deliberately.** Put familiar information early and new or important information late. Use end-stress when appropriate. Use short sentences, questions, fragments, inversions, appositives, and repetition sparingly.

**Maintain parallelism.** Keep series, paired constructions, repetitions, and ellipses parallel in form and function.

**Avoid forced style.** A pattern must sound natural. Do not create an inversion, fragment, absolute construction, question, or interrupting modifier merely for variety. Forced variety is a defect.

---

## Pass 2 workflow

1. Identify the paragraph's controlling idea (carry it forward from Pass 1 if available).
2. Number the sentences.
3. Identify each sentence's function: context, claim, evidence, explanation, contrast, transition, summary, or emphasis.
4. Identify current sentence structures and any repeated rhythms.
5. Look for problems:
   - monotonous sentence openings
   - weak or overused *to be* verbs
   - expletive constructions (*there is, it is*)
   - buried main clauses
   - unclear logical relationships
   - non-parallel series
   - missed opportunities for emphasis
   - too many long sentences without relief
   - too many short, flat sentences
6. Recommend only the patterns that solve a real problem. Limit to the strongest 2–5 changes.
7. Explain why each recommended pattern fits.
8. Apply the changes to produce the final paragraph.

---

## How to select a pattern

Use patterns as tools, not templates. First decide what the sentence needs:

- consolidation (combine related short sentences)
- contrast (oppose ideas sharply)
- emphasis (stress a key word, phrase, or clause)
- rhythm (relief from monotony, or deliberate cadence)
- transition (link ideas with explicit logic)
- clarification (rename, define, sharpen)
- relief (a short sentence after a long one)

Then select the pattern that fits. Recommend a pattern only when you can explain why it helps.

## Compound sentence patterns

### Pattern 1 — Compound sentence with semicolon, no conjunction

**Structure:** Independent clause; independent clause.

Use to join two short, closely related independent clauses. Useful when a paragraph has several short sentences with parallel ideas that combine into one stronger sentence.

**Use when:** ideas are closely related; both deserve equal weight; combining short sentences creates more force; no explicit connector is needed.

**Checks:** Both clauses must be complete sentences. Ideas must be closely related. Do not use merely to avoid a period.

### Pattern 1A — Compound sentence with semicolon and conjunctive adverb

**Structure:** Independent clause; however, independent clause.

Connectors: *however, hence, therefore, thus, then, moreover, nevertheless, likewise, consequently, accordingly*.

Use when the relationship between clauses needs explicit signaling.

**Checks:** Semicolon before the connector; comma after the connector is often used (optional depending on rhythm and clarity); the connector must accurately express the relationship.

### Pattern 1B — Compound sentence with coordinating conjunction

**Structure:** Independent clause, coordinating conjunction independent clause.

Coordinators: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.*

Coordinator functions:
- *for* — reason or cause
- *and* — adds or continues
- *nor* — continues a negative thought
- *but* — exception or contradiction
- *or* — alternative
- *yet* — contrast despite obstacle
- *so* — consequence or result

**Checks:** Use the coordinator that matches the logical relationship; comma with a coordinating conjunction between independent clauses; do not pair a semicolon directly with a coordinating conjunction in this pattern.

### Pattern 1C — Compound sentence with three or more independent clauses

**Structure:** Independent clause; independent clause; independent clause.

Use to connect three or more closely related complete statements in a deliberate chain.

**Use when:** each clause deserves equal weight; rhythm benefits from a linked sequence; ideas are parallel or cumulative.

**Checks:** each clause must be independent; avoid overuse — long chains can become heavy.

### Pattern 2 — Compound sentence with elliptical construction

**Structure:** Independent clause; elliptical independent clause.

Echoes Pattern 1 but omits a verb or phrase in the second clause because it would needlessly repeat the verb or phrase from the first.

**Use when:** two clauses share the same verb or phrase; omitting repetition creates balance and concision; the omitted words are obvious.

**Checks:** each clause still functions as independent even with implied words; omitted verb matches exactly in form and tense; structure remains parallel and complete; use a semicolon if no conjunction, comma if a coordinating conjunction joins them; if awkward or unclear, do not use.

### Pattern 3 — Compound sentence with explanatory statement after colon

**Structure:** Independent clause: independent clause.

The colon signals that the second clause explains, amplifies, or clarifies the first.

**Use when:** the second clause explains or amplifies the first; the first clause creates expectation for explanation.

**Checks:** both clauses must be complete; do not use a colon unless the second statement relates directly to the first. Patterns 1, 2, and 3 are not interchangeable.

## Series patterns

A series can reduce wordiness, combine short related sentences, provide examples, or create rhythm. All items must be parallel in grammatical form and function.

### Pattern 4 — Series without conjunction

**Structure:** A, B, C.

Commas between items, no final conjunction. Quick staccato rhythm; all items equal.

**Use when:** several items have equal weight; the sentence needs speed or compression; no final item should receive special emphasis.

**Check:** all items must be parallel.

### Pattern 4A — Series with repeated conjunctions

**Structure:** A and B and C; or A or B or C.

Conjunction between all items slows the rhythm and gives each item weight.

**Use when:** each item deserves individual emphasis; rhythm should feel deliberate.

**Checks:** usually do not use with more than three items; keep all items parallel.

### Pattern 5 — Series of balanced pairs

**Structure:** A and B, C and D, E and F.

Two, three, or four pairs with a conjunction between items in each pair.

**Use when:** paired words or phrases create rhythm; the sentence needs orderly progression; pairs can be balanced against each other.

**Checks:** read aloud to test rhythm; pairs should be structurally balanced; sequence should feel ordered, not arbitrary.

### Pattern 6 — Introductory series of appositives with dash and summarizing subject

**Structure:** Appositive, appositive, appositive — summary-word subject verb.

Summary words: *such, all, those, this, many, each, which, what, these, something, someone*.

**Use when:** compressing several related descriptions; you want a stylized emphatic opening; appositives prepare for a summarizing main clause.

**Checks:** commas between appositives; dash after the series; summary word at the start of the main clause; appositives parallel and related; use sparingly — highly stylized.

### Pattern 7 — Internal series of appositives or modifiers

**Structure:** Subject — appositive, appositive, appositive — verb.

Use dashes or parentheses to set off the interruption. Parentheses make the information less important; dashes make it more prominent.

**Use when:** an internal series renames or describes something already named; the interruption adds useful information; the main sentence remains clear.

**Checks:** pair of dashes or parentheses; sentence must remain complete if the internal material is removed; appositives rename, modifiers describe; modifiers must closely follow the word they describe.

### Pattern 7A — Single appositive or appositive pair as interruption

**Structures:**
- Subject, appositive, verb.
- Subject — appositive — verb.
- Subject (appositive) verb.

**Punctuation effects:**
- commas — neutral clarification
- dashes — dramatic emphasis
- parentheses — quiet aside

**Check:** use paired punctuation — two commas, two dashes, or two parentheses.

### Pattern 8 — Dependent clauses in a pair or series

**Structures:**
- If..., if..., then subject verb.
- When..., when..., subject verb.
- Subject verb that..., that..., that...

A pair or series of dependent clauses at the beginning or end. Clauses must be dependent, parallel, and tied to the main clause.

**Use when:** summarizing several conditions, situations, or provisions; constructing a multi-part thesis sentence; introducing or concluding a paragraph.

**Checks:** two may be enough — three is not required; arrange in increasing impact when possible; usually limit to two or three; do not force.

## Repetition patterns

Repetition can echo key words, emphasize important ideas, unify sentences, and create coherence across a paragraph. Avoid meaningless repetition of weak words.

### Pattern 9 — Repetition of a key term

**Structure:** Subject verb key term, repeated key term plus modifier.

Repetition may be introduced by a comma or dash.

**Use when:** a word is important enough to echo; repetition reinforces a main point; the repeated word can carry a modifying phrase.

**Checks:** the repeated word must be worth repeating; avoid bland words like *good, thing, important*; the phrase attached to the repeated word must not be a full independent clause (would create a comma splice).

### Pattern 9A — Same word repeated in parallel structure

**Structure:** Repeated key word in the same sentence position or grammatical slot.

Forms include: repeated adjective or adverb in parallel phrases; repeated preposition in a series; repeated noun as object of different prepositions; repeated modifying word in phrases beginning with different prepositions; repeated intensifier; repeated verb or related word form.

**Use when:** repetition strengthens rhythm; parallel structure improves cohesion; the repeated word marks a central idea.

**Check:** repetition must clarify or emphasize — not sound lazy.

## Appositive and modifier patterns

### Pattern 10 — Emphatic appositive at the end after colon

**Structure:** Independent clause: appositive.

The colon introduces a climactic renaming or restatement.

**Use when:** you want to repeat an idea (not just a word); the sentence should build toward a forceful final naming; the final phrase deserves emphasis.

**Checks:** words before the colon must form a complete sentence; after the colon, use a word or phrase — not a full independent clause (if it is a complete clause, consider Pattern 3 instead).

### Pattern 10A — Appositive after dash

**Structure:** Subject verb word — appositive.

A short emphatic appositive at the end after a dash. A dash usually works better for short appositives; a colon for longer ones.

**Use when:** the appositive is brief and climactic; the sentence needs a sharper ending.

**Checks:** must be a true appositive; do not use a dash to separate two complete thoughts (avoid dash splice).

### Pattern 11 — Interrupting modifier between subject and verb

**Structure:** Subject, modifier, verb.

A modifier (word, pair, or phrase) interrupts between subject and verb.

**Punctuation effects:**
- commas — neutral interruption
- dashes — dramatic signaling
- parentheses — aside or whisper

**Use when:** the modifier gives useful information; the interruption clarifies or emphasizes; dramatic signaling is appropriate.

**Check:** punctuation marks must come in pairs.

### Pattern 11A — Full sentence as interrupting modifier

**Structures:**
- Subject — full sentence — verb.
- Subject (full sentence) verb.

Interruption may be a statement, question, or exclamation.

**Use when:** the interruption is important to understanding the main sentence; the aside creates a useful rhetorical pause.

**Checks:** use with restraint; do not put a period before the second dash unless the interruption is a quotation; a question or exclamation may take its normal punctuation inside; parentheses make the interruption less important, dashes more prominent.

### Pattern 12 — Introductory or concluding participial phrase

**Structures:**
- Participial phrase, subject verb.
- Subject verb, participial phrase.

Present participles often end in *-ing*; past participles often end in *-ed/-d* (with many irregular forms).

**Use when:** the participial phrase naturally modifies the subject or action; the phrase adds context, manner, cause, or result.

**Checks:** avoid dangling participles; the participial phrase must clearly modify the intended noun.

### Pattern 13 — Single modifier out of place for emphasis

**Structure:** Modifier, subject verb.

Move a modifier away from its normal position for stress.

**Use when:** the modifier deserves stress; moving it improves rhythm; the sentence remains clear.

**Checks:** use a comma when needed to avoid misreading; watch for adverbs that could be mistaken for prepositions; helps avoid awkward split infinitives in formal writing; do not force the movement.

## Inversion patterns

Inversions change the usual order. They add variety and invisible stress, but can sound forced. Use sparingly and only when they fit the surrounding prose.

### Pattern 14 — Prepositional phrase before subject and verb

**Structures:**
- Prepositional phrase, subject verb.
- Prepositional phrase verb subject.

Establishes context, setting, or emphasis.

**Checks:** a comma may or may not be needed — rhythm and clarity decide; a prepositional phrase cannot stand alone as a sentence.

### Pattern 15 — Object or complement before subject and verb

**Structure:** Object/complement, subject verb.

Move direct object or subject complement before subject and verb for emphasis.

**Use when:** fronting creates graceful stress; the sentence sounds natural in context.

**Checks:** read aloud; use sparingly; avoid forced or gimmicky inversions.

### Pattern 15A — Complete inversion

**Structure:** Object/complement/modifier, verb subject.

Reverses normal order more fully.

**Use when:** dramatic or poetic emphasis is appropriate; context supports a more stylized sentence.

**Checks:** use rarely; less suited to business, laboratory, or highly technical prose; do not let the structure call attention to itself.

## Other patterns

### Pattern 16 — Paired constructions

Structures include:
- not only subject verb, but also subject verb
- just as subject verb, so too subject verb
- the more subject verb, the more subject verb
- the former subject verb, the latter subject verb
- if not phrase, at least phrase
- either…or, whether…or, both…and, neither…nor
- so…that, such…that, as…as, not so…as

Use for comparison, contrast, balance, or logical pairing.

**Checks:** pair means two — supply both halves; what follows each half must be parallel; put both conjunctions in logical places; *if not…at least* joins individual grammatical units, not full independent clauses.

### Pattern 16A — Paired construction for contrast only

**Structures:**
- This, not that.
- Not this, but that.

Use to show reversal, contrast, or correction.

**Use when:** the sentence needs a sharp contrast; one idea replaces or corrects another; the reversal should be clear.

**Checks:** keep both sides parallel; use punctuation (commas, dashes, parentheses) to mark the break.

### Pattern 17 — Dependent clause in a sentence slot

**Structures:**
- Dependent clause as subject + verb.
- Subject verb + dependent clause as object or complement.

Dependent clause functions as a noun. Common starters: *who, whom, which, that, what, why, where, when, how.*

**Use when:** a dependent clause naturally names the subject, object, or complement; the sentence needs variety without losing clarity.

**Checks:** the dependent clause must not stand alone; do not put a period before or after it in a way that creates a fragment.

### Pattern 18 — Absolute construction

**Structures:**
- Noun/pronoun + participle, main clause.
- Main clause, noun/pronoun + participle.

An absolute construction has no grammatical connection to the rest of the sentence but adds contextual or descriptive detail.

**Use when:** it naturally adds background, circumstance, or descriptive emphasis; the phrase improves variety without sounding forced.

**Checks:** always punctuate; comma at the beginning or end; if in the middle, paired commas, dashes, or parentheses; do not toss in merely for style.

### Pattern 19 — Short simple sentence for relief or dramatic effect

**Structure:** Subject verb.

A short sentence can provide intense clarity, especially after several long sentences.

**Use when:** summarizing what came before; creating contrast; providing transition; giving relief after complexity; creating dramatic emphasis.

**Check:** brevity alone is not dramatic — placement creates the effect.

### Pattern 19A — Short question for dramatic effect

**Structures:**
- Interrogative question?
- Statement turned into a question?

**Use in five possible places:** introduction, topic sentence, within a paragraph for variety, between paragraphs as transition, conclusion.

**Checks:** use carefully and sparingly; the question must arouse curiosity, stimulate interest, or lead the reader into a specific idea; do not scatter questions randomly.

### Pattern 20 — Deliberate fragment

**Structure:** Fragment.

A deliberate fragment can create dramatic effect, but it must be intentional.

**Use when:** the fragment creates emphasis; the fragment summarizes or echoes a key idea; the fragment produces a controlled pause.

**Checks:** must serve a purpose; must not be accidental; use Patterns 19, 19A, and 20 sparingly and precisely.

## Combining patterns

Combine only when the combination improves the sentence. Use sparingly. Examples:

1. **Patterns 3, 4, 9A** — colon + series + repeated key term.
2. **Patterns 9, 11** — repetition + interrupting modifier.
3. **Patterns 17, 10, 5** — dependent clause as complement + final appositive after colon + balanced pairs.
4. **Patterns 4, 9, 9A, 15A** — series without conjunction + repetition + introductory apposition + inversion.
5. **Patterns 1, 4, 4A, 9** — compound sentence + series + repetition.
6. **Patterns 6, 8, 9, 19A** — introductory appositives recast as dependent clauses + repeated key term + question for dramatic effect.
7. **Patterns 1, 14, 15A** — compound sentence + prepositional phrase + inversion before subject-verb.
8. Pair of dependent clauses as direct objects + paired words + series without conjunction + interrupting modifier with dashes + repetition in parallel structure.
9. **Patterns 11, 11A** — full-sentence interrupting modifier + another type of modifier.
10. Long, involved compound sentence without a conjunction followed by a deliberate fragment with a repeated keyword, then a fragmentary question.

---

## Coordinator checks

Use coordinators precisely:

- *for* — reason or cause
- *and* — addition or continuation
- *nor* — continues a negative thought
- *but* — exception or contradiction
- *or* — alternative
- *yet* — contrast despite obstacle
- *so* — consequence or result

Do not use a coordinator if it misstates the logical relationship.

---

## Scientific and academic mode

When scientific mode is active, favor clarity, precision, and restraint. Use patterns to clarify logic and emphasis — not to decorate prose.

Prefer precise verbs: *suggests, indicates, supports, tests, defines, distinguishes, links, constrains, regulates, alters, is required for, is sufficient to*.

Avoid inflated or AI-sounding language unless requested. Avoid em dashes by default in scientific prose unless requested or needed for a chosen pattern. Avoid Patterns 6, 15A, and 20 unless they fit the surrounding prose naturally.
