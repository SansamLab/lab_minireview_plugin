## Voice summary

The author's voice is **restrained, mechanistic, and claim-driven**. It usually starts from a concrete biological process, identifies a precise knowledge gap, and moves toward a testable model, experimental approach, or interpretation.

**Strongest version:** names molecular actors, experimental context, perturbations, and readouts.

**Weakest version:** slips into generic grant inflation such as *pivotal*, *profound*, *dynamic interplay*, *shed light*.

The style favors explicit logic, functional definitions, contrastive reasoning, and formal punctuation over conversational flourish.

## Core voice rules

### 1. Build the paragraph around a biological or experimental claim

Start with the process or problem, not with a broad flourish. Preferred openings name the concrete biological process, system, or experimental problem under study.

**Evidence:** field-specific process and system terms recur throughout the corpus (core process terms appear in roughly 16–22 of 22 files).

**Preferred:**
> Accurate [process] requires cells to coordinate [component A] with [component B], [state], and [transition].

**Avoid:**
> In today's rapidly evolving biomedical landscape, understanding [process] is more important than ever.

### 2. Use problem → gap → model → test → significance organization

Default structure for grant and manuscript prose:

1. Name the biological process or problem.
2. State the unresolved question or limitation of current models.
3. State the model or hypothesis.
4. Describe the experimental test or readout.
5. Explain the interpretation or broader significance.

**Evidence:** classified as a VOICE pattern because it appears across both grant and manuscript prose. Grant/research-plan documents use tighter sentences than papers (17.4 mean words per sentence vs. 22.8 in articles/preprints), but the same conceptual progression recurs across content types.

### 3. Prefer mechanistic verbs over inflated verbs

Use verbs that specify causality, perturbation, measurement, or inference: *regulate, determine, test, define, identify, recruit, bind, activate, suppress, deplete, promote, restrict, coordinate, measure, show, support, distinguish*.

**Evidence:** *determine* in 22/22 files, *test* in 20/22, *identify* in 20/22, *measure* in 17/22, *mechanism* in 20/22, *bind* in 14/22, *coordinate* in 8/22.

**Prefer:**
> We will test whether depletion of [factor X] advances [activity Y] and determine whether this change alters [process Z] through [pathway]-dependent mechanisms.

**Avoid:**
> We will leverage a powerful platform to unlock the intricate interplay between [process A] and [process B].

### 4. Use contrast to sharpen the argument

Distinguish related mechanisms using contrastive transitions: *although, however, whereas, in contrast, at the same time*. Use these to separate established knowledge from the gap, one mechanism from another, recruitment from activation, or cell-culture findings from in vivo tests.

**Evidence:** *although* appears 100 times in 18/22 files, *however* 78 times in 20/22, *whereas* 29 times in 9/22, *in contrast* 34 times in 13/22.

**Good pattern:**
> Although core [machinery] has been defined biochemically, how [system] allocates [capacity] across [contexts] remains poorly understood.

### 5. Define jargon functionally when writing for mixed audiences

For NIH-style or broadly scientific audiences, define technical terms before relying on them. Definitions should be compact and functional, not tutorial-like.

**Evidence:** the corpus repeatedly defines or contextualizes its core technical terms in grant documents. This pattern is strongest in grants and weaker in specialist manuscripts.

**Preferred:**
> [Term] is a [functional definition] that normally [default behavior] but can [conditional behavior] when [condition].

**Avoid:**
> [Term], which is a fascinating and complex class of [things], plays a crucial role in [broad outcome].

### 6. Keep uncertainty scientific and disciplined

Use scientific uncertainty markers: *suggests, supports, consistent with, raises the possibility, we hypothesize, we propose, may, will determine*. Avoid conversational hedges such as *maybe, sort of, kind of, I guess*.

**Evidence:** *suggests* appears in 14/22 files, *consistent with* in 14/22, *hypothesize* in 12/22.

### 7. Use punctuation to make logic explicit

Prefer commas, colons, and semicolons over em dashes. Use colons to introduce explanations, lists, or mechanisms; use semicolons to join tightly related independent clauses. Avoid gratuitous em dashes, especially in grant prose.

**Evidence:** grant DOCX files contained very few em dashes; colons and semicolons were common. Low em-dash use and preference for formal punctuation classified as VOICE pattern.

### 8. Avoid decorative rhetoric

The voice is strongest when prose is concrete. Replace inflated phrasing:

- *shed light on* → *test, define, determine, show, explain*
- *delve into* → *examine, test, analyze*
- *utilize* → *use*
- *intricate interplay* → *mechanism, relationship, coordination, coupling*
- *unlock/unveil/unravel* → *identify, define, determine, test*

**Evidence:** flagged LLM-like terms appear in the corpus but are weak voice signals: *interplay* in 6/22 files, *shed light* in 4/22, *pivotal* in 5/22, *profound* in 6/22, *utilize* in 9/22, *uncover* in 13/22. These should not be default style choices.

## Opening patterns

**Voice patterns to keep:**

Open by naming the biological process or problem. Typical first moves:
- define the process
- establish why it matters for genome stability, development, or disease-relevant biology
- identify what remains unknown

**Evidence:** recurring corpus openings use field-level process frames that name the process and its coordination with a related process or state.

**Avoid:**

- Anecdotal openings.
- Rhetorical questions.
- Broad societal frames.
- "In today's..." openings.
- Praise-first or sycophantic openings.

**Platform patterns to ignore:**

Do not infer personal voice from grant headers (SPECIFIC AIMS, SIGNIFICANCE, INNOVATION, APPROACH, RESEARCH STRATEGY). These are platform conventions.

## Sentence patterns

**Voice patterns to keep:**

- Moderate-to-long explanatory sentences, especially in manuscripts. Published/preprint prose averages **22.8 words per sentence**; grant prose averages **17.4 words per sentence**.
- Short declarative claim followed by longer mechanistic explanation. Use the short sentence to establish the problem or conclusion, then use the next sentence to specify mechanism, evidence, or consequence.
- Functional parenthetical clarification for abbreviations, aliases, examples, figure references, and technical qualifiers. Parentheses are NOT used for jokes or casual asides.
- Lists organized by biological order or experimental logic: step A → step B → step C; early → late stage; cultured cells → whole organism; perturbation → readout → interpretation.

**Platform patterns to ignore:**

- Extremely dense journal sentences caused by figure legends, methods, author contributions, or citation conventions.
- Highly compressed grant-specific sentences caused only by page limits.

**Borderline patterns:**

- Very high formality may be partly platform-driven. Preserve it for grant/manuscript work; soften it for recommendation letters, teaching materials, and public-facing summaries.

## Structural patterns

**Voice patterns to keep:**

- Organize by causal or biological sequence: step A → step B → step C; early → late stage.
- Use functional headers when they help reviewers navigate: Significance, Innovation, Approach, Rationale, Expected Outcomes, Potential Problems, Recent Progress.
- Use contrast paragraphs to distinguish alternative explanations.
- Make the logic explicit: why the question matters, why the experiment tests it, and what each outcome would mean.

**Evidence:** voice profile classified biological sequence organization and problem → gap → hypothesis/model → test → significance as VOICE patterns across grants and manuscripts.

**Platform patterns to exclude:**

- Numbered Specific Aims structure is grant convention.
- Heavy figure references and citation scaffolding are manuscript conventions.
- Reference lists, acknowledgments, author contribution language are not voice evidence.

**Borderline patterns:**

The *long-term goal / objective / central hypothesis / rationale / impact* structure is partly NIH convention and partly explicit grant style. Use it for NIH-style documents when appropriate; do not force it into manuscripts or public-facing summaries.

**Evidence:** *long-term goal* in 10/22 files, *central hypothesis* in 5/22, *rationale* in 4/22, *impact* in 12/22.

## Tone markers

**Voice patterns to keep:**

- Formal, mechanistic, restrained tone.
- High directness: state what is unknown, what will be tested, and why it matters.
- Scientific uncertainty rather than conversational hedging.
- Disagreement framed as incompleteness of current models or knowledge gap, not personal criticism.
- Minimal humor in scientific and grant writing.

**Adjust by context:**

For public-facing material: keep mechanistic precision but reduce density and define jargon earlier.

For letters: keep restraint but allow more human warmth and direct endorsement.

## Formatting habits

**Keep:**

- Oxford comma in lists.
- Conventional capitalization, except for required gene/protein symbols and formal headings.
- Bold or visually marked section labels only when they help navigation in grant-style documents.
- Colons and semicolons for explicit logic.

**Avoid:**

- Emoji in formal scientific writing.
- All-caps emphasis outside required headings or nomenclature.
- Excessive bolding.
- Decorative em dashes where commas, colons, parentheses, or semicolons work.
- Markdown code blocks unless the user asks for code or technical workflow.

**Evidence:** no emoji or casual typographic markers in corpus; no code-block habit. Low em-dash use and formal punctuation classified as VOICE.

## Format-specific guidelines

### Grant / research plan prose

**Corpus evidence:** 12/22 files; ~71,121 words; mean sentence length **17.4 words**; 32.1% of sentences ≤12 words; 5.5% ≥35 words.

**Use:**
- compact claim-driven paragraphs
- explicit gap statements
- central model or hypothesis language when appropriate
- direct experimental verbs: *test, determine, measure, define, identify*
- outcome/interpretation language
- functional headers

**Avoid:**
- excessive protocol detail unless requested
- inflated impact claims
- *comprehensive understanding* unless scope justifies it
- repeated *therefore/thus/furthermore* transitions

**Template pattern:**
> [Process/problem] requires [mechanistic coordination]. However, [specific unresolved question]. We hypothesize that [actor/process] [mechanistic role]. We will test this model by [perturbation/readout]. These experiments will determine whether [specific inference], establishing [bounded significance].

### Manuscript / article prose

**Corpus evidence:** 8/22 files; ~86,079 words; mean sentence length **22.8 words**; 25.9% of sentences ≤12 words; 13.3% ≥35 words.

**Use:**
- more specialist terminology
- tighter connection to data and figures
- interpretive transitions: *consistent with, suggesting that, together, these data support*
- precise distinctions between result, interpretation, and model

**Avoid:**
- broad grant-style future-impact claims
- overexplaining standard terms for specialist readers
- overusing *novel, important, significant* where the data should carry the claim

### Methods / protocol prose

**Corpus evidence:** 1/22 files; ~5,705 words; mean sentence length **18.4 words**. Low confidence due to single-file sample.

**Use:**
- procedural clarity
- numbered or chronological steps when helpful
- reagent, timing, instrument, condition specificity
- direct imperative or passive procedural phrasing depending on venue

### Research document / internal scientific prose

**Corpus evidence:** 1/22 files; ~9,362 words; mean sentence length **17.5 words**. Low confidence.

Use the grant voice as default, but allow more explicit planning language, alternatives, rationale.

### Emails, Slack, blog posts, casual messages

**No corpus evidence.** Do not claim derived patterns for greetings, sign-offs, emoji, humor, or short-message style. When asked to draft these formats, extrapolate from the scientific voice: direct, clear, restrained, specific, with warmth adjusted to audience. **Mark output as extrapolated.**

## Warnings and limitations

- The corpus is large but genre-skewed toward grants, research plans, manuscripts, protocols.
- Email, Slack, blog, classroom, casual-message patterns cannot be reliably inferred.
- Some files are coauthored, revised for funder/journal conventions, or include boilerplate; do not overfit to journal metadata, author lists, references, required grant headings.
- Some grant-style language overlaps with LLM-overrepresented wording. Treat **mechanistic specificity** as the strongest marker of authentic voice.
